# Preachers, church support, and Becker’s transference

**The Bible supports funding gospel workers. It does not establish that every mature congregation must keep one permanent paid preacher. Becker helps explain why a church might feel that it must have one anyway.**

I read the entire [local PDF of *The Denial of Death*](</home/ed/Downloads/The Denial of Death.pdf>): all 144 PDF pages, including the foreword, all eleven chapters, and the notes and references. I also checked the Festus slides and the current contradiction notes. The Bible evidence is saved in [evidence.json](/home/ed/search/preacher-role-pay-and-transference-2026-10-01/evidence.json).

The earlier version of this answer needed correction. It used “transference” to mean moving Old Testament priesthood into Christianity. That is not Becker’s meaning. It also claimed that salaries are forbidden, that Timothy and Titus were needed only because churches had no mature members, and that their work necessarily ended once elders were appointed. Those claims went beyond the passages.

## What a preacher is, and whether one man is needed

A preacher proclaims the gospel [the Christian good news]. A teacher explains it. An evangelist works to spread it. These activities can overlap in one person. None of those descriptions, by itself, proves that every church must appoint one permanent paid man.

Preaching is not confined to outsiders. Acts 15:35 describes Paul and Barnabas, with many others, teaching and preaching in Antioch. Timothy is told both to preach the word and to do an evangelist’s work. Calling him a preacher is therefore reasonable; treating the word as proof of a modern job arrangement is a separate step. [Acts 15:35](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts+15%3A35&version=YLT), [2 Timothy 4:2–5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Timothy+4%3A2-5&version=YLT).

Your strongest point is that **a duty belonging to the church does not automatically require one man to do most of it**. The passages spread the work:

| Work | Who is told to do it? |
|---|---|
| Teach and encourage believers | Gifted teachers; elders able to teach; believers teaching and encouraging one another. |
| Teach younger women | Titus 2:3–5 specifically gives older women this work. |
| Bring up children in the Lord’s instruction | Ephesians 6:4 addresses fathers. It does not reserve this work for a preacher. |
| Build up the congregation | Ephesians 4:11–16 names distinct workers and describes every part contributing. |
| Appoint elders | Titus receives this specific commission in Titus 1:5. The verse does not give every person called a preacher exclusive control of appointments. |

Sources: [Titus 1–2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Titus+1-2&version=YLT), [Ephesians 4:11–16; 6:4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ephesians+4%3A11-16%3B6%3A4&version=YLT), [Colossians 3:16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Colossians+3%3A16&version=YLT), [1 Timothy 3:2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Timothy+3%3A2&version=YLT).

But shared responsibility does not mean identical gifts. First Corinthians 12:29 asks whether all are teachers; the expected answer is no. A congregation can have many mature members while some are especially able to teach, organize, or reach outsiders. Funding one person can give him time to do useful work. It need not give him a higher spiritual rank. [1 Corinthians 12:28–30](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+12%3A28-30&version=YLT).

The practical question is: **What work needs this person’s time, and why is this arrangement useful here?** A church should be able to answer with actual work and actual needs. “A church must have a preacher” repeats the claim that needs proving.

A capable member can offer to teach without acquiring a special spiritual status. But having a gift is not permission to take control of the gathering or appoint oneself over everyone. First Corinthians 14 allows many contributions while requiring order. Shared work and recognized leadership can coexist. [1 Corinthians 14:26–33, 40](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+14%3A26-33%2C40&version=YLT).

## Timothy and Titus do not prove your whole proposed explanation

Your idea that churches should grow capable of teaching and caring for themselves has support. Second Timothy 2:2 tells Timothy to entrust teaching to faithful people who can teach others. Hebrews 5:12–14 criticizes people who should have grown beyond needing basic instruction. These passages support growing capability. They do not say that distinct teaching work disappears once a congregation matures.

The claim that Timothy and Titus were sent **only because no mature members were present** is not established:

- Paul gives Timothy a specific reason for staying in Ephesus: stop certain people teaching differently. That is a problem of false teaching, not simply a lack of teachers. First Timothy 5 also discusses elders already leading and teaching.
- Titus is left to finish what is lacking and appoint elders in each city. The required candidates must themselves have mature character and enough understanding to encourage and correct others. A lack of appointed elders does not prove a lack of every mature believer.
- Acts 20 presents Ephesian elders responsible for shepherding the church. It also warns that destructive teaching can arise from within their own number. Having leaders does not remove every need for outside help.
- Titus 3:12 tells Titus to come to Paul when Artemas or Tychicus arrives. This supports mobile work and possible replacement. It does not state that all such work ends permanently when local members mature.

Sources: [1 Timothy 1:3–7; 5:17–22](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Timothy+1%3A3-7%3B5%3A17-22&version=YLT), [Titus 1:5–9; 3:12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Titus+1%3A5-9%3B3%3A12&version=YLT), [Acts 20:17, 28–32](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts+20%3A17%2C28-32&version=YLT), [2 Timothy 2:2; Hebrews 5:12–14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Timothy+2%3A2%3BHebrews+5%3A12-14&version=YLT).

Nor does Titus’s commission establish that a permanent hired preacher must direct every future appointment of elders or deacons. First Timothy 3 gives qualifications and requires deacons to be tested. It does not prescribe that employment arrangement. Acts 6 shows the congregation choosing seven men for a service task, followed by the apostles appointing them. It is an example of shared selection; the passage does not explicitly call those seven deacons or settle every later appointment procedure.

## What the Bible establishes about support

**The case for supporting gospel workers does not depend only on Jesus’s mission instructions to the seventy-two, or only on verses about elders.**

| Passage | What it establishes |
|---|---|
| 1 Corinthians 9:6 | Paul and Barnabas have a right to be supported rather than earning their living through other work. |
| 1 Corinthians 9:7–14 | Paul argues from soldiers, farmers, shepherds, the law, and temple service. Verse 14 states that those proclaiming the gospel should live from it. |
| 2 Corinthians 11:8–9 | Other churches funded Paul’s work at Corinth. The text calls what he received “wages.” Brothers from Macedonia supplied his needs. |
| Philippians 4:15–18 | A particular congregation repeatedly sent help for Paul’s needs. |
| 3 John 5–8 | Believers ought to support traveling brothers working for Christ’s name. These workers are not identified as the seventy-two. |
| Galatians 6:6 | A person being taught should share good things with the person teaching. The instruction is not restricted to elders. |
| 1 Timothy 5:17–18 | The direct recipients of “double honour” are elders, especially those laboring in teaching. The wage and ox examples give the honour a material side. The phrase does not set an exact two-times salary. |

Sources: [1 Corinthians 9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+9&version=YLT), [2 Corinthians 11:8–9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Corinthians+11%3A8-9&version=YLT), [Philippians 4:15–18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Philippians+4%3A15-18&version=YLT), [3 John 5–8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=3+John+5-8&version=YLT), [Galatians 6:6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Galatians+6%3A6&version=YLT), [1 Timothy 5:17–18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Timothy+5%3A17-18&version=YLT).

Paul sometimes worked to support himself and sometimes accepted support. First Corinthians 9 expressly distinguishes having the right from choosing not to use it. Second Thessalonians 3:9 makes the same distinction. His willingness to waive support does not erase the right he argues for.

Ordinary sharing could meet a worker’s needs. Acts 4 describes distribution according to need. But this cannot reduce every support passage to ordinary membership in one local sharing group. Paul received support from other congregations while serving Corinth. Other passages connect support specifically with teaching and gospel labor.

The earlier answer also misused Second Corinthians 9:7. The chapter concerns a collection for the saints and the giver’s willing attitude. It does not discuss salary arrangements or say that a church’s agreed regular payment to a worker is forbidden. A freely given contribution can fund an agreed payment. [2 Corinthians 9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Corinthians+9&version=YLT).

Therefore:

- Supporting a worker enough to free him from other paid work has biblical support.
- Regular payments can be a practical way to supply that support. The passages do not prescribe a modern salary schedule, but silence about that schedule does not by itself forbid it.
- “I enjoy evangelizing” does not by itself establish that a chosen congregation owes someone lifelong maintenance.
- The cited passages do not establish a guaranteed lifetime claim against one particular church. They also do not forbid long-term support for continuing useful work.
- The duty to support worthy work is a different claim from the duty to create one permanent paid preacher post in every congregation.

## What Becker adds

The following applies Becker’s account to your church question. Becker did not study the Festus congregation. These are explanations to examine, not proven motives of named people. His sweeping claims about mental illness and sexuality are not being treated here as established facts or used to diagnose anyone.

### A person can need a preacher for two different reasons

There can be a practical need: someone has knowledge, time, skill, or experience that the congregation needs.

There can also be a need for reassurance: someone must stand in front of us and make us feel that our church, our beliefs, and our lives are right.

Both needs can exist together. Real skill does not prevent someone becoming the focus of unhealthy dependence. Conversely, a person’s need for reassurance does not prove that the teacher’s work is useless or his teaching false.

### Death is part of a larger problem of worth

In chapters 1–4, Becker connects awareness of death with the desire for a life that matters. People want to belong to something that outlasts their bodies and makes their effort worthwhile. He calls the systems that give them this place “hero systems” [shared ways of deciding which lives and actions matter]. Religious membership, a nation, a career, and a scientific movement can all serve this purpose.

For a believer, “our church follows God’s will” can carry more weight than an ordinary opinion. It can help carry the meaning of an entire life. A challenge to one teaching or arrangement can therefore feel much larger than its actual size.

Applied to this case, changing the preacher arrangement might raise frightening questions: Were our earlier choices wrong? Did we mistake a tradition for a command? Who can now assure us that we are faithful?

Those questions do not prove that the arrangement is false. They help explain why a weak argument may receive unusually fierce protection.

### Transference puts that reassurance into a person

In chapter 7, Becker uses transference more broadly than simply repeating feelings toward a parent in therapy. It means placing unusually large hopes, fears, trust, and authority onto another person, so that this person seems able to make our lives safe and meaningful.

In a church, the change can be subtle:

- “He helps me understand this passage” becomes “I cannot safely understand it without his approval.”
- “He encourages our faith” becomes “Without someone in his position, our congregation is spiritually incomplete.”
- “I should consider his correction” becomes “If he rejects me, God must reject me.”

The issue is the added power given to his approval. Expertise can justify listening carefully. It does not make a person the final source of your worth or your standing with God.

### Fear of life matters alongside fear of death

Becker’s account is not just “people are scared of dying.” People can also fear living as responsible individuals: making decisions, being mistaken, disappointing others, and acting without complete certainty.

A leader can narrow a huge, uncertain world into a manageable task: satisfy this person. His judgment becomes a clear measure of whether you are doing well.

That offers what Becker describes as a safe way of being heroic. A member can feel faithful through attending, paying, agreeing, and receiving approval, while leaving harder judgment and action to the leader.

This makes your question about shared duties especially important. A preacher can perform useful work while the arrangement also lets members avoid developing their own judgment and gifts. Renaming payment “support” does not settle whether that avoidance happens.

### The dependence can run both ways

Becker does not describe only a powerful man controlling weak followers. Drawing on writers about groups, he explains how a group can create the leader it needs. Its central person need not even be unusually forceful or charming.

Members may need a person who appears certain. That person may need members who confirm that he is specially called and necessary. Their needs can reinforce each other:

- The congregation receives reassurance and a visible model of faithfulness.
- The preacher receives purpose, recognition, and a place in something lasting.
- His livelihood may then become tied to keeping that role secure.

No deliberate fraud is required. A sincere man can become trapped by expectations of certainty. Admitting error may threaten both his own identity and the group’s confidence. Members can therefore help create the very authority that later limits them.

This is why “the preacher is paid” is too shallow an explanation. The harder question is what both sides receive from keeping him necessary.

### A limited person cannot carry unlimited meaning

Chapter 8 examines romantic love as a replacement for religious meaning. One partner becomes responsible for the other’s complete happiness, worth, and rescue from insignificance. The partner cannot carry that burden.

The church application is similar. A teacher can explain, encourage, and correct. He cannot remove every uncertainty, make every decision safe, or make a congregation incapable of error.

When a church demands that he do those things, his ordinary human limits can feel like betrayal. Both sides may protect an image of him that prevents honest correction.

The burden is not only on members. A minister may feel unable to be uncertain, rest, change roles, or leave. He has become responsible for holding together more than any person can hold.

### Christian faith can either loosen or tighten the dependence

Chapter 9 is crucial. Becker does not simply dismiss religion as cowardice. He argues that faith can free people from making nearby human approval the final judge of their lives. It can support freedom, dignity, and hope beyond the limits of a particular leader.

But he also says that religion can strengthen the old dependence when human authorities receive divine backing. In this PDF, that discussion is on page 82.

The difference is practical:

- A teacher helps you answer to God with understanding and responsibility.
- Or his own approval becomes the required proof that God approves of you.

Calling someone a servant of God does not establish the second claim. Respect for leadership is compatible with leaders having limits, being corrected, and having their work reviewed. First Peter 5:1–3 tells elders to shepherd without lording it over those entrusted to them.

Becker’s religious argument is about what faith can do in a life. It is not a proof that a particular church’s claims are true.

### Removing one man does not automatically solve the problem

Dependence can attach to an office, a committee, a founder’s memory, or the group itself. The individual minister may be replaceable while the position still functions as the sign that the congregation is legitimate.

A church with several elders can still make dissent feel like betrayal of God. A church with no salaried minister can still train members to need collective permission before thinking or acting.

Nor is the critic exempt. Chapter 7 discusses negative transference [giving an enemy an oversized place in one’s inner life]. Chapter 6 examines Freud’s movement as a source of lasting meaning for its founder and followers. A person can build his worth around defeating preachers, mastering arguments, or being the one who sees through everyone else.

That does not refute his argument. It means the argument still needs evidence, and the critic should not mistake independence from one man for freedom from every need for approval.

Chapter 11 rejects the promise of a person freed from all limits and dependence. Becker’s aim is not a church of isolated people who need nobody. The useful question is whether our relationships help us live more openly and responsibly, or make us more fearful and controlled.

## What this changes in the Festus assessment

The clearest problem is printed slide 24: it moves from examples of workers serving churches to the claim that every congregation ought to have a minister serving with it.

If “minister” means any Christian who serves, this does not prove the need for a special resident worker. If it means one specially designated preacher in every congregation, the universal requirement needs its own scriptural argument. Examples of receiving help do not establish that every congregation must always receive the same arrangement.

The contradiction notes also need more care. Some listed clashes are not contradictions:

- A shared Christian duty can coexist with different gifts and particular workers.
- God’s calling and a congregation recognizing someone’s ability can coexist.
- Serving one congregation for a time is compatible with going elsewhere later.
- Using “minister” broadly and narrowly requires clarity, but does not automatically make every Christian a ruler.

The pay argument is stronger when it names an actual claim. Second Corinthians 11:8 calls Paul’s support wages. Thus a blanket claim that support can never be payment needs a convincing explanation. But a claim that churches should not own or dominate their workers is compatible with paying them. Paying a person does not make him your property.

Becker adds a question about the claim that a minister cannot retire except by death. Continuing to serve Christ is different from keeping the same funded role for life. If ending or reviewing that role is treated as rejecting God’s calling, a practical arrangement may be acquiring authority beyond its demonstrated purpose. That is a possibility to examine, not proof of the author’s private motive.

## Questions that would reveal the actual arrangement

1. What work requires this person’s dedicated time? Who else can already do it?
2. Does he help other members become capable teachers and servants?
3. Can a qualified member disagree with him from the text without being treated as disloyal to God?
4. Can his work, authority, and support be reviewed without spiritual threats?
5. If he leaves, does the church lose useful help, or does it think it loses its legitimacy before God?
6. Are members still responsible for work they can do, or does funding him become their substitute for doing it?
7. Can the church change this arrangement when its needs change?

None of these alone proves transference. Together they distinguish ordinary respect and useful support from a person or office carrying more spiritual authority than the evidence warrants.

Your strongest challenge is therefore: **Where does the need for teaching and the right to support become a command that every congregation must maintain one special permanent preacher? And does that arrangement develop the members’ responsibility, or make them dependent on his approval?**
